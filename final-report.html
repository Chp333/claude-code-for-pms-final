Then it stopped asking four of them at all.

What happened, plainly

One small timing change met an old scoring rule, and quiet responders could never climb back.

1

The wait got shorter

4.2 cut the time to answer a request from 90 to 60 seconds, so callouts would move on faster.
2

Slow yeses became misses

Steady responders who answer in about a minute started running out of time.
3

Misses were scored as refusals

The system scored a miss as harshly as a "no", and never let a score recover over time.
4

They fell off the list

A low score means you're asked last, which in practice means never. Their neighbours got the work.

It wasn't a soft August: the week before release (3 Aug) was the best week in the data, and the drop starts on release day itself.

How it happened

A trap with no exit

Every step makes the next one worse, and nothing in the code ever lets a responder out.

The 4.2 ranking weights weren't the cause. 4.2 actually reduced how much past answers count (0.40 → 0.25). The trap was the scoring underneath.

Module 1 · Origin story

A miss cost the same as a "no", and misses exploded

PMYour prompt
What is the problem with product that needs to be fixed
01-origin-story/prompts.md · #1

Finding: in the code, a missed request costs −0.12, exactly like a turn-down, and only a "yes" (+0.08) ever raises a score. After the cut to 60 seconds, misses jumped from 2–6 a week to 38.

Module 2 · Super hearing

Customers felt it before the data showed it

PMYour prompt
What would you identify as the main bug root cause
02-super-hearing/prompts.md · #7

Finding: the root cause is dispatch() scoring a miss as a refusal (−0.12 vs +0.08 for a yes) with no recovery. The 4.2 cut to 60 seconds then demoted responders permanently. It's not the ranking weights.

The four handler interviews pointed the same way: "coat on, one boot on" when the callout was already gone.

Module 4 · X-ray vision

The code had no way back

PMYour prompt
According to my findings in the code, for someone who's gone quiet, they would need to ___.
04-x-ray-vision/prompts.md · #13

Finding: to climb back, a quiet responder would need to be asked and say yes, many times over. But a low score means they're rarely asked, and there's no recovery over time and no reset.

Replaying the code's own scoring showed it: everyone started 4.2 between 0.80 and 1.00. The four hit 0 because they missed 53–64% of their requests after 4.2, compared with 10–18% for everyone else.

The fix · design rules

Keep what 4.2 got right. Remove the trap.

Two goals that must stay solved

✅ Nearest first: the closest responder is asked first. The 4.2 weights stay.

✅ Fast hand-off: no request ever stays open more than 60 seconds. No rollback to 90.

How the skill keeps it from happening again

4.2 shipped without anyone checking it against its own goals

1 · UnderstandRead the brief and the build; list every goal and constraint
2 · Write criteriaHappy path, what must not happen, what-if cases, goal checks
3 · PM approvesNo test runs until you agree. This is where DR-06 was caught.
4 · Test the buildClick through it like a user; only observed results count as a pass
5 · Findings docVerdict, defects with steps to reproduce, gaps in the brief
How 4.2 shippedWith the qa-testing skill
Checked against the release's own goals✕ No. Nearest-first got worse and nobody saw.✓ Every goal (nearest first, 60-second limit) becomes a test
"What if…" cases✕ None. Slow responders weren't considered.✓ Edge cases for every requirement: no signal, mis-taps, gaming the score
Agreed acceptance criteria✕ None written down✓ 108 criteria, approved by the PM before testing
Contradictions in the brief✕ Shipped as written✓ Raised as questions first (DR-06 vs DR-11, merged)
Evidence✕ "August is always soft"✓ A findings document: verdict, defects, steps to reproduce
